01|notice|A permit does not fail loudly. Here is the New York City Department of Buildings, in its own service notice, dated January seventeenth, twenty twenty four. Abandoned applications, expired permits and applications are not valid after their expiration date. No hearing. No warning. The date passes and the paper stops meaning anything.
02|expiry|And it does not take a mistake. The same notice: a permit will expire where work has not commenced within twelve months from the date of issuance. A slow winter is enough.
03|price|The city also publishes what late costs. A thousand dollars a month for a late facade report. Five thousand a year for failing to file one at all. Penalties apply after the filing period ends, and nothing announces that the period has ended.
04|meter|In January twenty twenty four the department levied its first round of penalties on over four hundred property owners who missed one filing deadline. The commissioner's own advice: get those late reports in as soon as possible, because monthly penalties have already started, and will continue to rack up until we get compliance.
06|city|So we read the city's own open data this morning. Six hundred and five work permits went past their expiry date in the last thirty days. Collapse the renewal sequences and five hundred and forty two are real. Ask whether the city ever signed the job off, and four hundred and sixty three of them were still open work.
07|absence|That is the shape of it. The city publishes everything. The contractor finds out when it is already late. There is nothing in between, and that gap is the entire product.
08|read|Filings are prose, and prose can be read into dated obligations. One business name, one list of everything that is about to come due.
09|engine|Then the part that matters. Dates are arithmetic, so a deterministic engine makes the call, and no prompt reaches it. The model sits underneath it and does exactly one job: it reads the disposition text a clerk typed. It decides nothing.
10|pass|On a real plumbing contractor's real public filings: two hundred and thirty five items screened. Sixteen with a filing due and a draft already written. Forty needing one question answered. And a hundred and seventy nine held completely.
11|holds|Every held item names the check that held it. Eighty seven were the building owner's obligation and not this contractor's. Nine sit on jobs the city already signed off. Three were renewed by a later sequence. Those are the alarms a naive join on building and date would have raised, and every one of them would have been wrong.
12|prose|Here is the part arithmetic cannot do. Of five hundred and eighty one violations the city still lists as active with a comment typed into a free text field, a keyword list correctly closes five hundred and seventy four. It leaves seven. Paid invoice. Challenge approved. Each one already settled, where acting would mean paying twice. Seven is the only number worth a model.
13|question|And when the arithmetic is sound but a fact is missing, it stops and asks one question. This permit lapsed months ago. The city waives the penalty only if nothing was done on site after the date passed, and no dataset holds that fact. So it asks, once, and puts no send button on the screen at all.
14|console|This is what a person actually sees. Fifty three decisions, and above them the shape of the whole portfolio. Thirty three lapsed, three critical, eight due. The worst one named outright: twelve hundred and eight days late, at four three zero one Seventh Avenue, Brooklyn.
15|draft|When the rulebook does name the filing, the response is written, with the rule it rests on and the record it came from. Then it stops. Sending something on a contractor's behalf is not the agent's call to make.
16|gate|That is the gate, drawn. A hook cancels the send unless the engine returned a filing verdict, the item is still open, and a draft exists for that exact case. The approval is the one thing a person does.
17|veto|It is not a prompt asking nicely. Delete the hook and the suite goes red, because a test approves a case first and then calls send on a job the city already signed off. Put it back, and a hundred and sixty two tests pass.
